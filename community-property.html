Community Property Division in Louisiana

Identifying, valuing, and dividing marital assets and debts fairly across the Shreveport–Bossier City area.

Louisiana is one of a handful of community property states, which makes the division of assets and debts in a divorce work differently than it does in much of the country. The rules can be intricate, and small mistakes in classifying or valuing property can have lasting financial consequences. Christopher Stahl helps clients across Bossier City and Shreveport identify what belongs to the marriage, place a fair value on it, and divide it in an orderly way, whether that happens by agreement or through the court.

Community Property vs. Separate Property

The first task in any division is sorting what is community from what is separate, because only community property is split between the spouses.

Community property generally includes property acquired by either spouse during the marriage through their effort or skill, the income earned during the marriage, and things bought with community funds. Under Louisiana’s system, each spouse owns an undivided one-half interest in the community.

Separate property generally belongs to one spouse alone and is not divided. It typically includes:

  • Property owned before the marriage;
  • Property inherited by or given to one spouse individually during the marriage;
  • Property acquired with separate funds; and
  • Certain damages personal to one spouse.

Classification is not always obvious. Separate and community property can become mixed, and Louisiana law presumes that property possessed during the marriage is community unless a spouse proves otherwise. Tracing the source of funds is often where these disputes are won or lost.

Valuing the Property

Once property is classified, it must be valued so the community can be divided fairly. Some assets are simple to value, while others require documentation or a professional appraisal. Common items that call for careful valuation include:

  • The family home and other real estate;
  • Retirement accounts and pensions;
  • A business or professional practice;
  • Vehicles, furnishings, and other personal property; and
  • Bank, investment, and other financial accounts.

Because the value of an asset can be contested, having accurate figures and, where needed, qualified appraisers is an important part of protecting your share.

Dividing the Community

Spouses are free to reach their own agreement on how to divide the community, and a negotiated settlement often gives both people more control and a faster, less expensive result. When spouses cannot agree, a court divides the community property. The aim is an equitable allocation of the net value between the spouses; this does not always mean that every individual asset is cut in half, but rather that the overall division is fair. To balance the split, one spouse may keep certain assets while the other receives offsetting property or an equalizing payment.

What Happens to Debts

Division is not only about assets. Debts incurred during the marriage are also part of the picture, and community obligations must be accounted for alongside community property. Sorting out which debts are community and which are separate, and deciding who is responsible for them, is a key step. Keep in mind that how a court or settlement allocates a debt between spouses does not necessarily change what a lender can pursue, which is one more reason to address debts carefully.

How These Issues Fit Into a Divorce

The division of community property is usually resolved as part of the larger divorce, alongside questions of custody and support. Getting an early, organized handle on your finances, gathering records, listing assets and debts, and understanding what is community versus separate, puts you in a stronger position throughout the case.
