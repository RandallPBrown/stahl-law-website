What Is the DWI Cleansing Period in Louisiana?

By Christopher M. Stahl · Criminal Defense

If you are facing a DWI (Driving While Intoxicated) charge in Louisiana and you have a prior conviction in your past, one of the first things you need to understand is the “cleansing period.” It is one of the single most important factors in how a new charge will be graded and what penalties you could face.

In Louisiana, the DWI cleansing period is 10 years. This is the look-back window the State uses to decide whether a prior DWI conviction counts when grading a new offense. If your previous conviction falls outside that 10-year period, it generally cannot be used to enhance the new charge from a first offense to a second, third, or subsequent offense.

What the Cleansing Period Actually Means

The cleansing period is not about how long alcohol stays in your body, and it is not measured in hours. It is a legal time frame — ten years — that determines whether a past DWI can be stacked on top of a new one for sentencing purposes. The clock is measured between the relevant dates of the prior offense and the new offense under La. R.S. 14:98.

Here is a practical example. Suppose you were convicted of DWI eleven years ago and you are now arrested again. Because more than ten years have passed, that older conviction generally falls outside the cleansing period, and your new charge would typically be treated as a first offense rather than a second. That distinction matters enormously, because a first offense carries far lighter fines, a shorter potential license suspension, and less jail exposure than a second or third offense.

Why the Difference Between Offenses Is So Significant

Louisiana grades DWI penalties by offense level, and the jump between levels is steep:

  • First offense — a misdemeanor, with fines generally between $300 and $1,000 and the possibility of up to six months in jail (often suspended with community service and court-approved substance abuse and driver-improvement programs).
  • Second offense — harsher penalties, including a mandatory minimum jail term and fines that typically range from $750 to $1,000, plus mandatory programs.
  • Third offense — a felony, carrying a mandatory minimum of jail time, fines that can reach $2,000, and a lengthy license suspension.

Because the cleansing period can be the deciding factor in whether a charge is filed as a first or a repeat offense, getting the dates right — and challenging the prosecution where the records are wrong — can change the entire trajectory of your case.

What the Cleansing Period Does Not Do

It is critical to understand the limits of the cleansing period. Even if a prior conviction is too old to enhance a new charge, that older conviction is not automatically erased from your record. The cleansing period applies to charge enhancement and sentencing — it does not, on its own, wipe the prior DWI from your criminal history.

That means a past DWI can still surface on background checks and may still affect employment, professional licensing, and insurance, regardless of whether it falls inside or outside the ten-year window. Removing an old offense from public view is a separate process called expungement, which has its own eligibility requirements, including completing probation, satisfying all fines and fees, and remaining conviction-free.

Louisiana BAC Limits

A DWI charge usually turns on your blood alcohol concentration (BAC). In Louisiana, the legal limits are:

  • Drivers age 21 and older: 0.08%
  • Drivers under age 21: 0.02%
  • Commercial drivers: 0.04%

You can be charged with DWI if you are operating a vehicle at or above the applicable limit, and aggravating circumstances — such as a very high BAC or a minor passenger in the vehicle — can increase the penalties you face.

How an Attorney Can Help

An experienced DWI attorney does far more than count years. We review the circumstances of the stop and arrest, including the legality of the traffic stop, the administration of any field sobriety or chemical tests, and whether your rights were respected. Where the prosecution relies on a prior conviction to enhance a charge, we examine whether that conviction is actually within the cleansing period and whether the State can properly prove it. Errors and constitutional violations can be grounds to challenge the charge or seek a reduction.
