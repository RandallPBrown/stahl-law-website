A slip, trip, or fall can sound minor until it leaves you with a broken hip, a concussion, or a back injury that keeps you out of work for months. When a fall happens because a property owner failed to keep their premises reasonably safe, Louisiana law may entitle you to compensation for your medical bills, lost wages, and pain. What Is Premises Liability?

Premises liability is the area of law that holds property owners and occupiers responsible for injuries caused by unsafe conditions on their property. Slip-and-fall accidents are the most common type of premises claim, but the same principles apply to trip hazards, falling objects, inadequate security, and other dangerous conditions. The central question in any premises case is whether the person in control of the property failed to use reasonable care to keep it safe — and whether that failure caused your injury.

A Property Owner’s Duty of Care

Store owners, landlords, restaurants, and other businesses owe visitors a duty to maintain their premises in a reasonably safe condition and to warn of hazards they know about or should discover through reasonable inspection. That can mean cleaning up spills promptly, fixing torn carpet or uneven flooring, providing adequate lighting, clearing cluttered walkways, and addressing ice or water near entrances. When an owner ignores a hazard a careful business would have caught and fixed, and someone is hurt as a result, the owner may be liable.

Proving Negligence Under Louisiana Law

Louisiana sets a demanding standard for slip-and-fall claims against merchants. Under the state’s merchant-liability statute, an injured customer generally must prove three things:

  • A condition on the premises presented an unreasonable risk of harm that was reasonably foreseeable.
  • The merchant created the condition or had actual or constructive notice of it before the fall — in other words, the hazard existed long enough that a reasonable owner should have known about it.
  • The merchant failed to exercise reasonable care to correct the hazard or warn about it.

The notice requirement is often the hardest hurdle. Evidence such as surveillance footage, inspection and cleaning logs, incident reports, and witness statements can show how long a spill or hazard was present. Because this evidence can be lost or recorded over quickly, it is important to act fast and let an attorney request and preserve it.

Common Causes and Injuries

Slip-and-fall and other premises accidents frequently arise from wet or freshly mopped floors, uneven or broken flooring, loose rugs and mats, poor lighting, cluttered aisles, exposed cords, and ice or rainwater tracked near doorways. The injuries can be serious and lasting:

  • Fractures and broken bones, especially wrists, hips, and ankles.
  • Head injuries, including concussions and traumatic brain injuries.
  • Spinal cord and back injuries.
  • Sprains, strains, and torn ligaments.
  • Lacerations, bruises, and dislocations.

Comparative Fault in Premises Cases

Property owners often argue that the injured person was partly to blame — for example, by ignoring a warning cone or not watching where they were walking. Louisiana follows a pure comparative fault rule under La. C.C. art. 2323, which means your compensation is reduced by your share of the fault but is never eliminated entirely. If your damages are $50,000 and you are found 20% at fault, you can still recover $40,000. Because insurers use this rule to shift blame, having an advocate who can document the owner’s negligence is important.

The One-Year Deadline to File

Louisiana gives you a short window. Under La. C.C. art. 3492, you generally have only one year from the date of the fall to file a personal injury lawsuit. If you miss that deadline, the court will likely dismiss your claim regardless of how strong it is. The sooner you speak with an attorney, the sooner critical evidence like surveillance video can be preserved before it is erased.

What to Do After a Fall

If you are hurt in a fall, seek medical attention right away, even if you feel you can “walk it off” — some injuries surface later. Report the incident to the store manager or property owner and ask that a written report be made. Photograph the hazard and the surrounding area, and collect the names of any witnesses. Keep your shoes and clothing as they were, and save all medical records and bills. Then contact an attorney before giving any recorded statement to the property’s insurer.
