Theft Defense Lawyer in Bossier City

Aggressive defense against misdemeanor and felony theft charges across Bossier City and Shreveport.

Theft charges in Louisiana are a serious matter, and a conviction can carry significant consequences. Depending on the value of the property involved and your record, you could be facing jail or prison time, substantial fines, community service, and a permanent criminal record that affects employment and housing for years. That is exactly why anyone accused of a theft crime in North Louisiana should have an experienced Bossier City criminal defense attorney like Christopher Stahl on their side.

How Louisiana Defines Theft

Under Louisiana’s theft statute, La. R.S. 14:67, theft is the misappropriation or taking of anything of value that belongs to another — either without the other person’s consent, or by means of fraudulent conduct, practices, or representations. The law also requires intent: the State must show you intended to permanently deprive the owner of the property. If that intent is missing, the charge may not hold up.

Louisiana Grades Theft by Value — Not by "Grand Theft"

You may have heard the term “grand theft” on television, but it is not a Louisiana term. Louisiana does not use the “petty” or “grand” theft labels you see in some other places. Instead, R.S. 14:67 grades theft by the value of what was taken. The higher the value, the more serious the charge and the steeper the potential penalties.

Misdemeanor Theft (Value Under $1,000)

When the value of the property taken is less than $1,000, theft is generally a misdemeanor in Louisiana. A first-offense misdemeanor theft carries a maximum of six months in jail, a fine of up to $1,000, or both. Keep in mind that a misdemeanor theft can be elevated to a felony if the defendant has prior theft convictions, so even a “minor” charge should be taken seriously.

Felony Theft (Value of $1,000 or More)

Theft becomes a felony when the value of the property is $1,000 or more. Louisiana sets tiered penalties based on value:

  • $1,000 to under $5,000: up to 5 years imprisonment, a fine of up to $3,000, or both.
  • $5,000 to under $25,000: up to 10 years imprisonment, a fine of up to $10,000, or both.
  • $25,000 or more: up to 20 years imprisonment, a fine of up to $50,000, or both.

Because the dividing lines are based on dollar value, the precise valuation of the property is often a central issue. Challenging how the State values the property can sometimes reduce a felony exposure to a misdemeanor.

Related Theft Offenses in Louisiana

Louisiana law also defines a number of specific offenses that are related to general theft:

  • Shoplifting: Taking merchandise from a retailer with intent to permanently deprive the store of it. This is usually charged as theft and graded by value — a misdemeanor in many cases, but a felony when the value reaches $1,000 or more.
  • Theft of a motor vehicle: Graded by the value of the vehicle, following the same value tiers as general theft under R.S. 14:67.
  • Illegal possession of stolen things (R.S. 14:69): Knowingly possessing, receiving, or concealing property you knew or should have known was stolen, also graded by value.
  • Carjacking (R.S. 14:64.2): Taking a vehicle from a person by force or intimidation. This is an automatic felony carrying 2 to 20 years without parole, probation, or suspension of sentence.
  • Robbery: Taking property directly from a person by force or intimidation. Simple, first-degree, second-degree, and armed robbery are all serious felonies, with armed robbery carrying some of the harshest penalties in the criminal code.
  • Fraud-related offenses: Forgery, bank fraud, credit-card fraud, and similar charges that involve obtaining something of value through deception.

Why You Need a Bossier City Theft Defense Attorney

If you have been charged with a theft crime in Bossier City or Shreveport — whether or not you actually committed the offense — it is important to get experienced legal guidance early. A defense attorney protects your constitutional rights while building the strongest possible defense. That work can include challenging the alleged value of the property, questioning whether the evidence shows intent to permanently deprive, examining how any search or stop was conducted, and negotiating with the prosecutor. Depending on the facts, the goal may be a dismissal, a reduced charge, a favorable plea, or a not-guilty verdict at trial.
