Child Custody Attorney in Bossier City & Shreveport

Protecting your relationship with your children and pursuing arrangements that serve their best interests.

Reaching a custody arrangement during a divorce or separation can be one of the hardest parts of an already difficult time. Parents, and sometimes the court, must work together to find a solution that fits the children, and a misstep can affect your family for years. Louisiana custody law is detailed, and the standards judges apply are often broad. Christopher Stahl has handled a wide range of custody matters across Bossier City and Shreveport and works to protect your bond with your children while keeping their needs at the center of the case.

Legal Custody vs. Physical Custody

In Louisiana, custody covers two related but separate ideas:

  • Physical custody — where the child actually lives and who handles day-to-day care such as meals, school, and routine.
  • Legal custody — the authority to make major decisions for the child, including those about education, medical care, and religious upbringing.

Types of Custody Arrangements

Shared Custody

Both parents share legal custody and split physical custody roughly equally, with the child spending about the same amount of time in each home. Louisiana courts favor this arrangement when it is workable, but it asks a lot: parents must live reasonably close together and cooperate closely on decisions.

Joint Custody

Parents share legal custody, but one parent has greater physical custody, meaning the child lives with that parent more of the time. This often takes the familiar form of a primary home with a regular visitation schedule for the other parent, though the schedule can be tailored to the child’s needs.

Split Custody

When there are multiple children, one parent may have custody of one child while the other parent has custody of another. Depending on the circumstances, child support may or may not be involved.

Sole Custody

Sole custody, where one parent holds legal and/or physical custody alone, is uncommon and generally reserved for situations where the other parent is found unfit, such as a history of violence, substance abuse, or child abuse or neglect. Even then, the non-custodial parent is often granted visitation and access to the child’s medical and school records, and may still owe support.

How Courts Decide: The Best Interests of the Child

When parents cannot agree, the judge sets the arrangement based on what serves the child’s best interests. Louisiana law directs courts to weigh a number of factors, including:

  • The emotional ties between the child and each parent;
  • Each parent’s capacity to give the child love, guidance, and continued education;
  • Each parent’s ability to provide food, clothing, medical care, and other needs;
  • How long the child has lived in a stable, suitable environment;
  • The permanence of the existing or proposed home as a family unit;
  • The moral fitness of each parent, as it affects the child;
  • The mental and physical health of each parent;
  • The child’s home, school, and community history;
  • The reasonable preference of the child, if old enough to express one;
  • Each parent’s willingness to support the child’s relationship with the other parent;
  • The distance between the parents’ homes; and
  • Who has primarily cared for the child up to this point.

No single factor controls, and judges have considerable discretion. Because these standards are open to interpretation, experienced advocacy can make a real difference in how your circumstances are presented.

Visitation Rights

Louisiana allows visitation for a non-custodial parent and, in appropriate cases, for certain third parties such as grandparents. Courts grant these rights when doing so serves the child’s best interests.

Reaching an Agreement

Whenever possible, it is better for parents to negotiate their own custody plan than to leave every detail to a judge. A workable agreement typically addresses where the child lives, how holidays and special occasions are divided, when each parent has access to or contact with the child, and child support. When parents present an agreement at the time of divorce, the court will usually approve it if it serves the child.

Modifying a Custody Order

Custody is not necessarily permanent. Parents can seek to modify an arrangement, but how hard that is depends on how the order came about. If the original plan was one the parents agreed to, a party generally needs to show a change in circumstances. If a judge imposed the arrangement after a contested hearing, the standard is higher, and the parent seeking change must show that the modification is in the child’s best interests. This is worth keeping in mind when you set up your initial agreement.
