Louisiana's Stand Your Ground Law Explained

By Christopher M. Stahl · Criminal Defense

Few areas of criminal law generate more confusion — or more misinformation — than the right to use force in self-defense. In Louisiana, the principles often grouped together as “Stand Your Ground” can be a complete legal justification, but only when very specific conditions are met. Understanding where the lines are drawn can protect you and your family and keep a justified act from turning into a criminal charge.

This article explains, in plain terms, what Louisiana law allows, the role of reasonable belief, and the limits you must respect. It is general information, not legal advice for a specific situation.

What the Law Says

Louisiana law recognizes that a person who is where they have a right to be has no duty to retreat before using force to defend themselves. Under La. R.S. 14:19 and 14:20, the use of force — including deadly force in certain circumstances — can be justified to prevent a violent or forcible felony, or to defend against an attacker who poses an imminent threat of death or great bodily harm.

A closely related principle is the “Castle Doctrine.” The law presumes that a person who uses force against someone unlawfully and forcibly entering their home, vehicle, or place of business held a reasonable belief that force was necessary. In other words, when an intruder breaks into your occupied home, the law starts from the position that you were justified in defending it.

When the Use of Force Is — and Is Not — Justified

Justification depends heavily on the facts. Courts weigh factors such as:

  • The nature and degree of the force used to prevent the threat;
  • Where the encounter took place;
  • Whether the aggressor had the apparent ability to carry out the threatened harm; and
  • The circumstances confronting the person who used force.

Just as important is what the law does not allow. Deadly force is not justified against an intruder who is simply retreating or leaving the premises — the threat must be ongoing. Force is not justified to stop someone who is withdrawing peaceably, and it is not justified to prevent a mere misdemeanor in a private dwelling. The threat that justifies deadly force must be a forcible felony or an imminent danger of death or serious injury.

The Role of Reasonable Belief

The central question in most self-defense cases is whether the person held a reasonable belief that force was necessary. This is judged from the perspective of a reasonable person facing the same circumstances — not with the benefit of hindsight. Facts that support a reasonable belief might include that the person was lawfully present, knew an intruder had unlawfully entered, perceived the intruder to be armed, faced an attempt to commit a forcible felony, or had previously been threatened or assaulted by the aggressor.

Because reasonableness is fact-specific, two cases with similar headlines can end very differently. The details — what was said, what was seen, where each person stood, and what a reasonable person would have feared — often decide the outcome.

Using These Rights Responsibly

Having the legal right to defend yourself is not the same as having unlimited license. You cannot use force to answer a threat that does not actually exist, and the right to stand your ground does not authorize aggression or retaliation. Deadly force in particular is reserved for situations where an attacker is dangerous and you reasonably believe you face imminent death or great bodily harm. In most encounters, the safest and wisest course is still to remove yourself from danger and call law enforcement when you can.

Why Legal Guidance Matters

If you have used force to protect yourself or your family, you may be confident you were justified — but prosecutors and police will evaluate the same events through a very different lens. An experienced criminal defense attorney can help preserve evidence, present the facts that establish your reasonable belief, and assert the legal protections Louisiana provides. Getting that guidance early can make the difference between a case that is dismissed and one that proceeds to trial.
