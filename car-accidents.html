What to Do After a Car Accident

The steps you take in the hours and days after a wreck can make or break your claim. As much as your injuries allow, try to do the following:

  • Call 911 and get medical care. A police report and prompt treatment create an official record. Some injuries — concussions, soft-tissue damage, internal trauma — don’t show symptoms right away.
  • Document the scene. Photograph vehicle damage, license plates, road conditions, skid marks, and any visible injuries. Get the other driver’s name, insurance, and contact information.
  • Gather witnesses. Names and phone numbers of anyone who saw the crash can be decisive later.
  • Don’t admit fault. Even a casual “I’m sorry” can be twisted into an admission. Stick to the facts when you speak with police.
  • Keep your records. Save medical bills, repair estimates, and notes about missed work and pain levels.

How Fault and Insurance Work in Louisiana

Louisiana is an at-fault (or “tort”) state. That means the driver who caused the crash — and that driver’s liability insurer — is responsible for paying the damages. Unlike no-fault states, you are not limited to your own coverage; you can pursue the at-fault party directly. Louisiana drivers are required to carry minimum liability insurance, but those minimums are often far too low to cover a serious injury, which is why uninsured/underinsured motorist (UM/UIM) coverage on your own policy can be so important.

Determining who was at fault is rarely as simple as the other driver admitting it. Insurers investigate aggressively to shift blame. An attorney can gather the police report, photographs, witness statements, traffic-camera footage, and — when needed — accident-reconstruction analysis to build a clear picture of what happened.

Common Car Accident Injuries

The value of a claim is closely tied to the nature and severity of the injuries. Crash victims in our area commonly suffer:

  • Whiplash and other neck and back injuries, including herniated discs.
  • Concussions and traumatic brain injuries, which can have lasting cognitive effects.
  • Broken bones in the arms, legs, ribs, and hips.
  • Spinal cord injuries that may cause partial or permanent disability.
  • Internal injuries and lacerations that require emergency treatment.
  • Psychological harm, such as anxiety or post-traumatic stress.

Because some injuries worsen over time, it is important to follow your doctor’s treatment plan and keep every appointment. Gaps in treatment give the insurance company an excuse to argue you weren’t really hurt.

Comparative Fault: What If I Was Partly to Blame?

You can still recover even if you share some of the blame. Louisiana follows a pure comparative fault rule under La. C.C. art. 2323. Your compensation is reduced by your percentage of fault, but it is never eliminated — even if you were mostly responsible. For example, if your damages total $100,000 and you are found 30% at fault, you can still recover $70,000. Insurers know this rule and will try to assign you as much blame as possible to shrink their payout, so it pays to have someone protecting your side of the story.

The One-Year Deadline to File

Louisiana gives you far less time than most states. Under La. C.C. art. 3492, you generally have only one year from the date of the accident to file a personal injury lawsuit. Miss that deadline and the court will almost certainly dismiss your case, no matter how strong it is. One year passes quickly while you’re recovering and negotiating, so it is wise to speak with an attorney early — well before the deadline closes — to preserve evidence and protect your rights.

Dealing With Insurance Adjusters

Remember that the adjuster works for the insurance company, not for you. Their job is to close your claim for as little as possible. Common tactics include calling while you’re still in shock, requesting a recorded statement, pushing a quick lowball settlement before you know the full extent of your injuries, and asking you to sign broad medical-records releases. You are not required to give a recorded statement to the other driver’s insurer, and you should never sign anything or accept a settlement without understanding what you are giving up. Once you accept, you generally cannot reopen the claim — even if your condition worsens.
