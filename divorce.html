How Divorce Works in Louisiana

To begin, you file a petition for divorce in the parish where you or your spouse lives. Louisiana recognizes two general paths: a no-fault divorce, which does not require proving wrongdoing, and a fault-based divorce, which does. Which path fits your situation depends on your circumstances, whether you have minor children, and whether you and your spouse can agree on the terms of your separation.

No-Fault Divorce

Most couples in Louisiana pursue a no-fault divorce. A judge may grant it once spouses have lived separate and apart for the required period:

  • 365 days, if there are children under 18 from the marriage; or
  • 180 days, if there are no minor children.

Unlike some states, Louisiana generally requires that one spouse physically move out of the marital home during this period. A no-fault divorce moves most smoothly when both spouses agree on the issues that surround it, such as custody and spousal support. Where you disagree, a judge decides, and contested matters are usually where time and expense add up.

Fault-Based Divorce

A fault-based divorce is available when a spouse has committed certain serious acts. Grounds recognized under Louisiana law include:

  • Adultery;
  • Conviction of a felony with a sentence of death or imprisonment at hard labor;
  • Physical or sexual abuse of a spouse or a child of the parties, even without a criminal conviction; and
  • A protective order or injunction issued during the marriage to protect a spouse or child from abuse.

In these cases, having your own attorney is especially important, both to prove the grounds and to safeguard your rights as the case proceeds.

Covenant Marriage

Louisiana is one of only three states, along with Arkansas and Arizona, that offers covenant marriage. Couples who choose it agree to premarital counseling and to stricter limits on divorce. A spouse in a covenant marriage cannot file for a simple no-fault divorce; instead, they must establish specific grounds, which can include adultery, a felony conviction, abandonment for at least one year, physical or sexual abuse, living separate and apart for two years, or living apart for a set time after a judgment of separation. Counseling is typically required before a petition is filed.

How Custody, Support, and Property Fit In

Divorce rarely stands alone. Several related questions are usually resolved as part of the same case:

  • Child custody and visitation — decided according to the best interests of the child, and often easier to settle by agreement than by leaving it to the court.
  • Child support — calculated under Louisiana’s guideline formula, which considers both parents’ incomes and the needs of the children.
  • Spousal support — potentially available before and after the divorce, depending on need, fault, and each spouse’s circumstances.
  • Community property — the assets and debts acquired during the marriage, which must be identified, valued, and divided.

Reaching agreement on these issues before you stand in front of a judge gives you far more control over the outcome. A judge’s ruling is final, and it may not land the way you hoped.

The Typical Steps

While every case differs, most divorces follow a familiar path: filing the petition, serving your spouse (who then has a limited time to respond), exchanging information about property and finances, negotiating or mediating any disputes, and, where necessary, a hearing before the judge. The process ends with a judgment of divorce signed by the court.

Why You Need Your Own Attorney

Even in a friendly divorce, your spouse’s lawyer works for your spouse, not for you. Having your own counsel means someone is documenting agreements, protecting your interests, and preparing you for each stage. The earlier an attorney is involved, the fewer surprises you are likely to face later.
