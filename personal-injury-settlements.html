How Are Personal Injury Settlements Paid Out?

By Christopher M. Stahl · Personal Injury

If you have been hurt because of someone else’s negligence, you deserve to be compensated — for medical bills, lost income, property damage, and the pain and stress that come with a serious injury. A settlement is often the most direct path to financial stability while you recover. Understanding how that settlement is actually paid out helps you set realistic expectations and negotiate from a position of knowledge.

One important reality to grasp early: the number on the settlement check is rarely the amount that lands in your pocket. Liens must be satisfied, costs are reimbursed, and attorney’s fees are deducted. Here is how the process typically unfolds.

Step 1: You Sign a Release Form

Once both sides agree on a final figure, you receive a release form. It spells out the terms of the agreement, including the payout amount, and confirms that you accept the settlement. Critically, signing the release also means you agree not to sue the at-fault party or their insurer for any additional damages from the same accident. Always review the release with your attorney before signing — never sign anything related to your injury case without having it examined first.

Step 2: Your Attorney Receives the Check

The insurance company issues a settlement check, usually payable to both you and your attorney. Your lawyer deposits those funds into the firm’s client trust account, where they are held until liens, costs, and fees are properly accounted for.

Step 3: Liens and Debts Are Negotiated and Paid

Injured people often owe money to medical providers and others out of their recovery. These debts — commonly called “liens” — are frequently negotiable. A good attorney works to reduce the amounts you owe so that more of the settlement stays with you. This back-and-forth with providers can take time, but it can meaningfully increase your net recovery. Once agreements are reached, your attorney pays the lienholders from the trust account.

Step 4: Costs and Attorney’s Fees Are Deducted

Next, your attorney is reimbursed for case expenses advanced on your behalf — things like court costs, medical-record requests, copying, and correspondence with the insurer. Personal injury lawyers typically work on a contingency fee, meaning they are paid a set percentage of the recovery. The fee agreement you signed at the outset spells out the order in which liens, costs, and fees are paid.

Step 5: You Receive the Final Check

After liens, costs, and fees are resolved, you and your attorney close the case. You receive a settlement packet with a copy of the release and an accounting of every disbursement. Once you review and sign the final paperwork, you receive your net settlement.

How Settlement Amounts Are Calculated

Two common methods are used to estimate the value of a claim:

  • Multiplier method. Your economic damages (medical bills, lost wages, lost earning capacity, and property damage) are added to your non-economic damages, which are estimated by multiplying the economic figure by a number — often between 1 and 5 depending on the severity and lasting impact of the injury. A typical accident is rated around 1.5; more serious cases earn higher multipliers.
  • Per diem method. A daily dollar value (often tied to your daily earnings) is assigned for each day you were affected by the injury, then added to your economic damages.

These are starting points, not final answers. Before making any demand, it is wise to discuss your circumstances with an attorney who can explain what a fair settlement looks like for your specific situation.

How Settlement Negotiations Work in Louisiana

Louisiana Civil Code article 3071 governs settlement agreements and allows disputes to be resolved amicably by compromise. In practice, the injured party demands a sum, the insurer counters lower, and the two sides negotiate. If the at-fault party or insurer offers an amount the injured person is willing to accept, the matter settles, the agreement is written and signed, and payment follows. If a fair offer never materializes, the next step is often filing suit or proceeding to trial.

Lump Sum vs. Structured Settlements

The most common option is a single lump-sum payment, with fees, costs, and liens deducted at once. A structured settlement instead pays you in installments over time, which can be useful for ongoing medical needs or lost income. Some claimants negotiate a hybrid — a lump sum for immediate expenses plus structured payments for future costs.

Are Settlements Taxable?

Compensation for physical injuries (including car-accident and slip-and-fall injuries) is generally not taxable, and reimbursement for medical costs is typically tax-free where no prior deduction was taken. By contrast, items such as punitive damages, interest, certain lost-wage awards, and emotional distress unrelated to a physical injury can be taxable. Because tax treatment varies by claim, confirm the details with a qualified professional.
